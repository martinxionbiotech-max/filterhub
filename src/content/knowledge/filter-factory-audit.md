A filter factory audit is often treated as a pass-or-fail inspection of paperwork. The auditor arrives, reviews certificates, walks through the plant, takes photographs and produces a score. The report looks substantial, but it may not answer the question that matters: can this site make the buyer's product consistently, and can it show how it knows?

The audit is one gate in the wider [supplier selection and control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/). It sits after a desk screen and before first-article approval and routine production. Its purpose is to corroborate what the supplier has claimed, observe controls in operation and identify risks that a document review cannot reveal. It is not a substitute for product testing, and a good audit cannot make an unsuitable specification suitable.

The audit decision should also be read against the intended commercial route. An OEM programme may involve custom tooling, packaging and documentation, while a wholesale range may rely more heavily on standard products and repeat supply. The [OEM and private-label service page](/oem/) describes the commercial scope; the audit still decides whether the site and process meet the buyer's technical and quality requirements.

This guide sets out what a buyer should verify and how to judge the evidence. The exact checklist should follow the product, process and applicable standards. The method should remain the same: define scope, follow the product and material, test the control system with evidence, and close findings with consequences.

## 1. Audit the Control System, Not the Showroom

A factory audit should be planned around control points. A clean meeting room, a polished display cabinet and a wall of certificates may create confidence, but they do not show whether a media lot can be traced to a finished filter or whether a change in adhesive would be approved before use.

The buyer should state the audit objective before the visit. Typical objectives are to confirm the production site and process, evaluate the quality management system in practice, verify traceability and release controls, assess capability for the proposed product, and determine whether the supplier can meet the buyer's change, audit and documentation requirements. Findings should map back to those objectives.

A control-focused audit also avoids the trap of scoring things that are easy to see. Housekeeping, signage and meeting-room hospitality are not equally important across product types. The auditor should spend time where the product is made, where materials are received, where nonconforming goods are held and where test records are created.

## 2. Define Scope, Criteria and Evidence Rules

The scope should identify the legal entity, site, buildings, production lines, shifts and product families covered. It should state whether the audit includes warehousing, labelling, testing, maintenance and any outsourced processes. A certificate scope can help define the quality-system boundary, but the buyer must still establish which site and process will make the purchased product.

The audit criteria should be agreed in advance. They may include the buyer's supplier requirements, the product specification, the quality agreement, applicable management-system standards and any regulatory requirements identified for the market. If ISO 9001 or IATF 16949 is used as a reference, the auditor should understand what the standard covers and how the supplier applies it to the proposed product. A certificate does not remove the need to inspect the actual control.

Evidence rules matter. The auditor should be able to select records rather than accept a prepared sample provided by the supplier. The audit should allow time to follow a finished-goods lot backward to material and test records, and to follow a material lot forward to affected finished goods. Photographs and document copies should be permitted where confidentiality allows, with the basis recorded.

## 3. Review Documents Before the Site Visit

The pre-audit review should establish what the supplier claims and where the auditor should look. Useful documents include the quality manual or system description, certificate and scope, process map, plant layout, control plans, product specifications, recent release records, calibration list, nonconformance log, change log, complaints and corrective-action records. The list should be proportional to the product risk.

The auditor should not read every procedure. The purpose is to identify gaps and contradictions worth testing on site. If a control plan lists an inspection frequency that the release records do not show, that is a question for the floor. If the change log contains entries without approval evidence, the auditor should follow one through the affected product records.

The buyer should also compare the site's product scope with the purchase. A supplier that is competent in a different filter family may have the same equipment and still lack experience with the relevant media, sealing method or test standard. The [air filter performance framework](/knowledge/air-filter-performance/) can help identify which performance characteristics need corroboration during the visit.

## 4. Open With a Process Walk and a Trace Request

The opening meeting should confirm the scope, the schedule, the confidentiality arrangements and the availability of production, quality and engineering staff. The auditor should ask for a short explanation of the process and then walk it in the direction of material flow. This makes it harder to present a selected story.

The first evidence request can be a recent finished-goods lot that represents the buyer's product family. Ask the supplier to show the lot identity, the quantity, the production date, the line and shift, the release decision and the test records. Then follow the lot back to material certificates, inspection records and process settings. If the trail breaks, ask why and record the point.

The second request is the reverse: choose a material lot received in a defined period and ask which finished goods were made from it. If the supplier cannot answer without an improvised search, the traceability system may be reconstructed after the fact rather than maintained as a control. The buyer should distinguish a slow but valid record search from an inability to establish the relationship.

## 5. Verify Quality-System Scope and Site Control

Management-system evidence should be read as a scope-based claim. ISO 9001 addresses quality management system requirements. IATF 16949 addresses automotive quality management within its defined eligibility and certification scope. ISO 13485 addresses medical-device quality management where that product framework applies. ISO 14644 addresses cleanroom classification and testing, not the performance classification of a filter.

At the audit, the buyer should see how the site translates those expectations into document control, competence, calibration, supplier management, design or process change, nonconformance and corrective action. The certificate may support the existence of a system, but the auditor should test its operation with records and interviews. An expired internal audit, a corrective action closed without evidence or a document-control system that cannot distinguish revisions are signs that the system is not active.

If the supplier claims a regulated or automotive scope, the auditor should ask which products, lines and customer requirements are included. The buyer should not assume that a certificate obtained for one product family applies to a new filter, a different site or a customer-specific requirement. The quality agreement should state the actual evidence and approvals the buyer requires.

## 6. Examine Incoming Materials and Sub-Tier Control

Material risk often enters before the filter is assembled. Media, adhesives, gaskets, frames, separators, coatings and packaging can change performance, durability or compatibility without changing the finished dimensions. The audit should test how the supplier identifies approved materials, verifies incoming lots, stores them and controls substitutes.

Ask to see the approved-material list for the product family and compare it with the receiving records. Then select a critical material and follow its identity through the production record. The auditor should look for a certificate of analysis or conformity where appropriate, a receiving inspection that matches the material's critical characteristics, lot identification and segregation of nonconforming material.

Sub-tier control should be visible in the supplier's change system. If a media supplier changes a grade, a gasket compound changes or a subcontracted process moves to another site, the buyer needs a path for notification and approval. The [filter quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) should make that path contractual and identify the evidence required for each class of change.

## 7. Walk the Critical Process Steps

The process walk should cover receiving, storage, preparation, forming or pleating, bonding or sealing, curing, assembly, testing, labelling and packing as applicable. The auditor should observe the actual production condition, not only a demonstration. If the line is stopped, ask for the setup sheet, last production record and changeover record.

For each critical step, connect the machine setting to the product characteristic. Pleat geometry, media tension, adhesive quantity, cure condition, gasket placement and frame alignment may all affect fit or performance. The auditor does not need to become the process expert. The auditor needs to see whether the supplier has defined the setting, monitors it and reacts when it drifts.

The walk should include the handling of rejected material and rework. Are defective units identified, segregated and dispositioned by an authorised person? Is reworked product re-inspected against the same criteria? Are records kept? The answers show whether the process controls the product or merely the paperwork.

## 8. Test Measurement and Release Capability

A release test is only useful if the measurement system is adequate. The auditor should review the calibration list, the traceability of standards, the method used, the environmental conditions where relevant, the operator competence and the way results are recorded. For critical measurements, the supplier should be able to explain how repeatability and reproducibility were assessed, consistent with the logic of AIAG MSA where that framework is applicable.

Ask the supplier to perform a routine check while the auditor observes. Select a characteristic that matters to the buyer's product and compare the result with the record. A measurement that cannot be repeated, an instrument used outside its range or a test result recorded without the sample identity should be treated as a control weakness rather than a clerical issue.

The decision rule for release should be explicit. If a result is close to a specification limit, the supplier should know whether measurement uncertainty is considered, whether the result is accepted, rejected or referred, and who decides. The first-article process described in the [first article inspection guide](/knowledge/filter-first-article-inspection/) should use the same measurement logic that routine production will use.

## 9. Review Nonconformance, Corrective Action and Change Control

The audit should select actual nonconformances, not only the procedure. Trace one from detection through containment, disposition, root-cause analysis and corrective action. Ask what product was affected, how the scope was determined, whether the customer was notified and how effectiveness was checked. A corrective action that closes with “operator retrained” without evidence of the underlying cause is usually incomplete.

Change control deserves a separate sample. Ask for recent changes in material, process, equipment, software, site or sub-supplier. For each change, the auditor should see the request, risk assessment, approval, validation and first-batch release evidence. If the supplier cannot show which customers were affected, the buyer cannot rely on the assurance that changes are controlled.

The [silent change guide](/knowledge/filter-change-management/) explains why notification is not the same as approval. The audit should test the supplier's ability to stop a change before shipment when the buyer's approval is required.

## 10. Assess Contamination and Cleanroom Controls Where Relevant

If the product is made, handled or packed in a controlled environment, the audit should verify how that environment is classified, monitored and maintained. ISO 14644 provides a framework for cleanroom classification and testing, but the class of the room does not by itself prove that a filter is clean, leak-free or suitable for the application.

The auditor should check entry controls, gowning, airlocks, pressure differentials, particle monitoring, cleaning records, maintenance and the handling of equipment that could introduce contamination. The supplier should be able to link the environmental records to the relevant production period. A certificate on the wall is not enough if the monitoring record cannot show the condition during the buyer's production run.

Where cleanroom controls are not relevant, the auditor should still consider contamination from packaging, storage, handling and rework. A filter can be damaged or contaminated after the production line by a process that is outside the cleanroom but still inside the supplier's control.

## 11. Evaluate Capacity From Demonstrated Output

Audit evidence should include a capacity claim that can be tested. Nameplate equipment capacity, theoretical cycle time and sales forecasts are not enough. Ask for recent output, downtime, changeover time, scrap, rework and quality holds. The pattern should show how much productive capacity remains after the losses the process actually experiences.

Overall equipment effectiveness provides a useful common language:

$$OEE=A\times P\times Q$$

where $A$ is availability, $P$ is performance against the rated output and $Q$ is quality yield. The result should be read with the product mix, shift pattern and measurement period. A high OEE on a simple product does not prove that the same capacity is available for a new filter with more process steps or tighter acceptance criteria.

Capacity should be connected to the buyer's forecast and the supplier's allocation commitment. The [commercial risk article](/knowledge/filter-commercial-risk/) explains how to test utilisation and concentration. The audit should record the evidence used, not just the supplier's capacity figure.

## 12. Sample Evidence and Score Findings Proportionately

An audit is a sample of a control system. The auditor cannot review every lot, every operator and every change. The report should state what was sampled and what the sample supports. This does not make the audit weak; it makes the conclusion honest. A finding can be valid even if it is based on one lot, especially when it reveals a systemic failure.

A useful finding separates observation, requirement and consequence. The observation is what was seen. The requirement is the standard, specification or agreed control. The consequence is why it matters to the buyer. This structure makes the finding reviewable and prevents the report from becoming a list of preferences.

| Audit area | Core question | Evidence to sample | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope and site | Is this the legal entity and site that will make the product? | Licence, certificate scope, organisation chart, site map | Unclear entity or production site |
| Incoming materials | Can approved materials be identified and controlled? | Approved list, receiving records, material lots | Substitution without approval |
| Process control | Are critical settings defined and monitored? | Control plan, setup sheet, in-process records | Settings not linked to product characteristics |
| Traceability | Can finished goods and materials be followed both ways? | Lot records, labels, test links | Records prepared after the event |
| Measurement | Are test methods and instruments adequate? | Calibration, MSA, raw data | Result without method or sample identity |
| Nonconformance | Is bad product contained and dispositioned? | Hold area, NCR, rework record | Unauthorised rework or release |
| Change | Are changes approved before production? | Change log, approvals, first-batch data | Customer discovered the change afterward |
| Capacity | Is output supported by demonstrated data? | Output, downtime, OEE, backlog | Nameplate capacity used as a promise |

## 13. Write Findings That Can Be Closed

The audit report should identify major, minor and observation-level findings, but the classification should not substitute for the buyer's risk decision. A major finding may be technical, systemic or commercial. A minor finding may become major if it affects a critical characteristic or recurs across lots. The buyer should define how each classification affects approval and shipment.

Each finding should cite the evidence that supports it, such as the sampled lot, record, observation or interview. A finding written only as an opinion is difficult for the supplier to correct and difficult for the buyer to defend. Where the evidence is limited, the report should say so and propose the additional check needed to reach a conclusion.

Corrective action should be specific. The supplier should state containment, root cause, correction, corrective action, responsible owner and completion evidence. The buyer should review effectiveness, not just accept a closure date. For a finding that affects product already shipped, the supplier should identify affected lots and the customer notification path.

The audit conclusion should state whether the site is approved, conditionally approved or not approved for the defined product and scope. Conditions should be measurable, such as completion of a traceability test, calibration of a specific instrument or submission of a change-control procedure. A general promise to improve is not a closure criterion.

## The practical position

The practical position is that an audit without the purchased product in scope is a supplier tour with better paperwork. We would audit the line that will run the buyer's part, follow an actual lot backward to materials and test records, and watch the reaction to a finding rather than score the reception room. A site can fail for this product and remain usable for another, so approval should be tied to the product family and process, not the company name. If the auditor cannot connect the checklist to the buyer's failure modes, the report grades presentation.

## 14. What this page does not claim.

This page is an audit-planning framework, not a certification standard, legal opinion or substitute for a competent on-site audit of the actual factory. It does not establish that any named supplier, site, certificate or process is compliant, and an audit finding is not a determination of product conformity. The buyer and its qualified auditors remain responsible for the audit scope, evidence, conclusions and any approval decision based on the actual factory and purchase.
