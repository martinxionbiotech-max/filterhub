Tooling and intellectual property are easy to postpone until after the first order. The supplier needs a tool, the buyer needs a sample, and the commercial discussion focuses on price and timing. By the time the relationship ends or a dispute begins, the tool may be at a supplier site, the drawings may have passed through several people and no one can show which version was approved.

The omission is expensive because tooling and technical data encode the buyer's product. A pleating tool, forming die, assembly fixture, test adapter, packaging plate, master sample or equipment program can determine whether a product can be reproduced, moved to another supplier or protected from unauthorised use. The [supplier selection and control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/) treats these assets as part of production control, not as a legal afterthought.

This guide sets out the practical ownership questions for a buyer sourcing filters from Chinese suppliers. It is not a substitute for Chinese legal advice. The objective is to make the commercial and technical record clear enough that qualified counsel can draft and enforce the right terms.

## 1. Start With an Asset Map, Not a Generic NDA

A non-disclosure agreement is useful, but it does not answer who owns a purchased tool, who may use a drawing after the relationship ends, or whether a supplier can use process knowledge learned during the project for another customer. Those questions require an asset map.

List every asset that is created, transferred or used in the project. The list may include product drawings, specifications, CAD files, test methods, control plans, tooling, fixtures, gauges, moulds and dies, machine programs, master samples, packaging artwork, labels and brand assets. It should also distinguish buyer background technology from supplier background technology and from new technology developed during the project.

For each asset, record who created it, who paid for it, where it is located, who can access it and what happens at the end of the relationship. This is more useful than a single clause saying “all intellectual property belongs to the buyer.” A broad clause may be difficult to apply to a supplier's pre-existing manufacturing know-how or to a jointly developed improvement.

## 2. Separate Ownership, Possession, Access and Use

Ownership and physical possession are different. A buyer may own a tool that sits in the supplier's factory. The supplier may possess the tool without having the right to use it for another customer. A buyer may own a drawing while the supplier owns the process knowledge needed to make the product efficiently.

The contract should address four separate interests:

- Title: who legally owns the tool, drawing, specification or other asset.
- Possession and location: where the asset is stored or installed and who controls access.
- Use rights: which products, customers, markets and time periods the supplier may use it for.
- Return or transfer: how the asset is returned, moved, copied or destroyed at exit.

Confusing these interests produces weak controls. A tooling invoice may show that the buyer paid for a tool, but it may not state title, marking, access or transfer. A confidentiality clause may protect a drawing, but it may not give the buyer a right to retrieve a physical fixture.

## 3. Make Ownership Explicit in the Contract

For buyer-funded tooling, the contract should state that title passes to the buyer on payment or another defined event, and should identify the tool by description, drawing number, serial number or asset register entry. It should state where the tool is held, who may use it, how it will be marked and what records the supplier must maintain.

For intellectual property, the contract should identify the buyer's pre-existing materials, the deliverables created for the project and the supplier's background know-how. It should state how ownership of project deliverables is assigned or licensed. If the supplier will create drawings, test methods or software for the buyer, the agreement should address the rights in those outputs rather than assuming that payment alone transfers them.

In China, copyright, patents, trade secrets and contractual rights operate through different legal mechanisms and evidence requirements. The practical approach is to use a written agreement with clear definitions, require written assignment where ownership is transferred, and keep records of the creation, delivery and acceptance of the asset. Registration can support a right, but it does not replace a clear contractual chain.

The [quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) should connect these asset rights to the product configuration. If a material or process change is approved, the associated drawings, specifications and tooling records should be updated under the same control system.

## 4. Control Drawings and Technical Data by Version

Drawings and specifications should be released as controlled documents, with a revision, date, owner and distribution list. The buyer should not rely on email attachments as the only record of the approved version. The supplier should acknowledge the revision it is using and should not produce to an older revision without written authorisation.

Access should be limited to people who need the data. The buyer can use secure transfer, watermarks, access logs and recipient lists where appropriate. The supplier should identify whether subcontractors, testing laboratories or software providers will receive the data and under what obligations. A supplier that cannot list the parties with access cannot control the disclosure.

Technical data should also have a retention and return rule. At exit, the buyer may require return or destruction of copies, confirmation from sub-recipients and transfer of the latest approved files. The supplier may need to retain some records for legal, quality or accounting reasons; the agreement should distinguish required retention from continued use.

## 5. Register and Mark Physical Tooling

Physical tooling should be listed in an asset register and marked in a durable but non-damaging way. The register should include the tool identification, the product it makes, the location, the owner, the date of manufacture or purchase, the maintenance history and the current status. If a tool is modified, the change should be linked to the relevant product approval.

Marking is not just for ownership. It helps prevent a tool from being used for the wrong product or from being confused with a supplier-owned tool. It also supports an audit: the buyer can select a finished-goods lot and ask which tool made it. If the answer cannot be linked to a register, the control is incomplete.

For tools that are difficult to mark, the supplier should use a labelled storage location, a serialised fixture record or another traceable method. Photographs can support the record but should not be the only evidence of identity.

## 6. Validate Tooling Before Production Approval

Tooling acceptance should be part of the first-article process. The supplier should demonstrate that the tool produces the specified dimensions and features, that it can run at the intended production rate and that it can be maintained without losing the approved characteristics. The first article should identify the tool and the process settings used.

The validation should include the initial condition, the running condition and the condition after a representative maintenance cycle where relevant. A tool that produces a good first part and then drifts after a few cycles is not production-ready. The [first article inspection guide](/knowledge/filter-first-article-inspection/) provides the acceptance structure; tooling validation supplies the process evidence.

If the tool will be transferred to another site or supplier, the buyer should require a transfer validation. The new site should demonstrate that the tool produces a conforming product with its own equipment, operators and process conditions. Ownership transfer alone does not prove capability.

## 7. Define Maintenance, Repair and Modification Rights

A tool changes over its life. Wear, repair, refurbishment and modification can affect the product. The contract should state who is responsible for maintenance, who pays for it, what records are kept, who approves a repair that could affect the product and how the buyer is notified.

The supplier should not be able to modify a buyer-owned tool for another product or to repair it in a way that changes the approved configuration without approval. The buyer should have access to maintenance records and the right to inspect the tool. If the tool is sent to a third party for repair, the same access and confidentiality obligations should apply.

For supplier-owned tooling that is dedicated to the buyer, the agreement should define the buyer's continuity rights. The buyer may not own the tool but may need assurance that it will not be diverted or scrapped while the supply relationship is active. The mechanism can be a licence, a reservation of capacity, a tooling bond or another commercial arrangement; the right solution depends on the risk and the negotiation.

## 8. Control Subcontracting and Third-Party Access

A supplier may need to send a tool to a specialist for coating, machining, repair or calibration. It may also use a subcontractor for part of the filter production. The buyer should know which parties will access the tool, drawings or technical data before production starts.

The supplier should remain responsible for the subcontractor's compliance with confidentiality, access and quality requirements. The buyer's audit rights should extend to the work that affects the product, and the change-control process should cover a move to a new subcontractor. A flow-down clause is useful only if the supplier can show that it has been applied.

If a subcontractor refuses access or confidentiality terms, the buyer should treat that as a supplier risk, not a procurement inconvenience. The buyer may need to approve the arrangement, find an alternative or adjust the scope of the work.

## 9. Scope Exclusivity and Confidentiality Realistically

Confidentiality obligations should identify the information covered, the permitted purposes, the excluded information, the recipients and the duration. Trade secrets require reasonable secrecy measures, so practical controls such as access restrictions, marking and need-to-know distribution matter. A confidentiality clause without operational controls is weak evidence of a protected secret.

Exclusivity is a different issue. A supplier may be asked not to make the same product for competitors, not to use the buyer's tooling for others or not to sell under the buyer's brand. The scope should be specific: product, market, customer, territory and duration. Broad restrictions may be difficult to enforce or may create commercial conflict.

Before agreeing to exclusivity, the buyer should consider whether it will provide enough volume to make the restriction workable. An exclusive arrangement with a supplier that also depends on other customers can create allocation risk. The commercial risk article linked from the pillar explains how capacity concentration should be assessed alongside exclusivity.

## 10. Plan the Exit Before the Relationship Begins

The exit plan should identify what happens to tooling, drawings, data, master samples, inventory and work in progress. It should state the notice period, the conditions for return or transfer, the verification method, the treatment of copies and the obligations that survive termination.

An exit is easier when the supplier has maintained the asset register and the buyer has kept its own copy of the approved documentation. The buyer should not assume that a supplier will produce a complete file years later. Periodic asset confirmations can reveal a missing tool, an unrecorded modification or a subcontractor that was never approved.

| Asset or right | Evidence to establish | Exit question |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer-funded tool | Purchase record, title clause, asset register, marking | Can the buyer retrieve, transfer or have it destroyed? |
| Supplier-owned dedicated tool | Contract, use restriction, capacity commitment | Can it be diverted while supply is active? |
| Product drawing | Revision-controlled file, acknowledgement, access list | Which copies exist and who holds them? |
| Test method or fixture | Specification, validation record, ownership clause | Can it be moved and revalidated? |
| Packaging artwork | Artwork file, approval record, brand licence | Who can use it after termination? |
| Master sample | Identification, storage condition, retention rule | Is it returned, retained or destroyed? |
| Subcontractor data | Flow-down agreement, access record | Who must delete or return copies? |

## 11. Quantify Tooling Cost and Capacity

Tooling decisions should not be based only on the quoted tooling charge. The cost per unit over the expected volume is one useful measure:

$$C_{\text{tool per unit}}=\frac{C_{\text{tool}}}{Q_{\text{planned}}}$$

where $C_{\text{tool}}$ includes acquisition, validation, maintenance and expected transfer costs, and $Q_{\text{planned}}$ is the volume over which the tool is expected to be used. A low tooling price can be a poor choice if the tool has a short life, requires frequent repair or cannot be transferred.

Capacity can be estimated from the tool and process:

$$Q_{\text{tool}}=\frac{H_{\text{available}}}{T_{\text{cycle}}}\times n_{\text{cavities}}\times OEE$$

where $H_{\text{available}}$ is available run time, $T_{\text{cycle}}$ is the cycle time, $n_{\text{cavities}}$ is the number of parts produced per cycle and $OEE$ reflects availability, performance and quality losses. The result should be verified with actual production data. A tool that can make one good sample per cycle may not have the same effective capacity after changeovers, maintenance and quality holds.

## 12. Keep the Evidence Practical

The buyer does not need a legal department on every project, but it does need a consistent record. Maintain a controlled drawing register, a tooling asset register, a signed ownership schedule, an access list and an exit checklist. Review the records at the same time as the quality and commercial review, not only when a dispute begins.

For a private-label programme, the packaging artwork and brand assets should be treated with the same discipline as the product drawings. The [private-label supply page](/private-label/) describes the commercial route; the asset controls here determine what the supplier may use and for whom.

The final test is transferability. If the buyer had to move the product to another qualified supplier, could it retrieve the tool, identify the approved revision, obtain the process records and validate the new site? If the answer requires the current supplier's goodwill and institutional memory, the buyer does not yet control the asset base.

## 13. Confirm Assets Periodically

An asset register is only useful if it is checked. The buyer should require a periodic confirmation of buyer-owned and dedicated tooling: location, condition, current use, maintenance status and access by third parties. The confirmation can be a signed statement supported by photographs and records, followed by physical verification during an audit.

The same review should confirm the drawing and specification distribution list. Ask whether any new subcontractor, testing laboratory or software provider has received technical data. Ask whether copies exist outside the controlled system and whether obsolete revisions have been withdrawn. These questions are easier to answer before a dispute than after a supplier has changed ownership or lost key staff.

When a discrepancy is found, the buyer should record it and set a correction date. A tool that is at a different address, a drawing that has been revised without notice or a missing master sample is a control issue even if production is currently acceptable. Periodic confirmation turns the exit plan from a hopeful clause into a maintained state.

## 14. What this page does not claim.

This page is a practical ownership and control framework, not legal advice under Chinese or any other law and not a substitute for a tailored contract reviewed by qualified counsel. It does not establish that any particular tool, drawing, patent, copyright, trade secret or contractual clause is valid or enforceable, nor does it guarantee that a supplier will return an asset. Ownership, access, transfer and enforcement depend on the actual agreement, evidence and applicable legal framework.
