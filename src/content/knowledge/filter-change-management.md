The most difficult supplier change is the one the buyer never sees. A filter can arrive in the same box, with the same part number and the same dimensions, while the media, adhesive, gasket compound, pleat geometry or production site has changed. If the supplier treats the change as an internal efficiency improvement, the buyer may learn about it only when a batch behaves differently.

Silent change is not an unusual event. Suppliers face material shortages, cost pressure, equipment replacement, subcontractor moves and customer-specific demands. Some changes are necessary and beneficial. The risk is not change itself; it is change that is not assessed against the approved configuration, not approved by the buyer and not verified before the affected product ships.

This guide covers the change-control system a buyer should require. It sits within the [supplier selection and risk-control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/) and connects the quality agreement to production control. The commercial route for an OEM programme is described on the [OEM and private-label service page](/oem/), but the control obligation begins before the purchase order is placed.

## 1. Treat Change as a Predictable Commercial Event

A supplier's cost structure creates incentives to substitute materials, change a process, consolidate production or find a second sub-supplier. Those decisions may be rational from the supplier's perspective. The buyer's control problem is that the supplier may see only the commercial benefit while the buyer carries the performance, regulatory and customer-approval consequences.

Change management should assume that changes will occur. It should define what the supplier must disclose, how quickly, to whom, with what evidence and with what approval status. It should also define what happens when a change is discovered after shipment. A system that relies on the supplier's goodwill to volunteer every change is not a control system.

The buyer should make the obligation specific to the purchased product. A general clause requiring “notification of changes” is weaker than a list of change categories, an approval form, a named contact and a release condition. The [quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) should turn the expectation into an auditable obligation.

## 2. Define Which Changes Require Approval

Not every change has the same consequence. A change to carton artwork may require commercial approval; a change to media grade may require technical and performance requalification. The buyer should classify changes by their potential effect on form, fit, function, performance, regulatory status, traceability and verification.

Change categories commonly include:

- Product design: dimensions, construction, pleat geometry, media area, frame, gasket or marking.
- Material: media, adhesive, gasket compound, coating, separator, packaging or labelling material.
- Process: bonding, curing, pleating, forming, cleaning, testing or inspection method.
- Equipment and tooling: new machine, modified tool, repaired mould or changed fixture.
- Site and organisation: new building, line, shift pattern, subcontractor, ownership or legal entity.
- Sub-tier: new material supplier, new grade from an existing supplier or a moved sub-supplier process.
- Software and records: test software, labelling system, traceability system or release-record format.

The list should identify which categories require prior written approval and which require notification only. When in doubt, the supplier should ask before shipping. A buyer should not create a category that allows the supplier to decide unilaterally that a technical change is “equivalent.”

## 3. Make Prior Approval a Condition of Supply

The default rule for a critical filter should be no shipment of affected product until the buyer has approved the change. Approval should be documented, dated and linked to the affected product, site and lot range. A supplier's internal engineering change notice is evidence of its decision, not the buyer's approval.

The quality agreement should state the consequences of an unapproved change. These may include rejection, containment, sorting, replacement, requalification cost, suspension of new orders or a requirement for independent verification. The exact remedy is a commercial and legal matter, but the control principle should be clear: an unapproved change is a nonconformance, even if the changed product happens to pass the original test.

The buyer should also control changes initiated by the buyer. A revised drawing, new packaging, changed test method or alternative material supplied by the buyer can create the same requalification burden. The buyer's internal change process should trigger the supplier's change process and update the approved configuration.

## 4. Require a Change Request That Can Be Assessed

A change request should identify what changes, why, when, where and what product or lot is affected. It should describe the current and proposed condition, the reason for the change, the risk assessment, the proposed qualification plan and the date the supplier intends to implement it.

The request should also identify the affected sub-tier suppliers and the evidence that their material or process remains within the approved requirements. If the change is a material substitution, the buyer needs the material specification, certificate, identity evidence and any performance data. If the change is a process or site move, the buyer needs a process comparison, equipment list, validation plan and first-batch data.

The request should include a proposed customer notification list. A supplier may have several buyers using the same product family; the change may affect them differently. The buyer should not be asked to approve a change for a product it does not purchase. The scope of approval should be explicit.

## 5. Assess Risk From Failure Modes, Not From Labels

The supplier may describe a change as “minor,” “equivalent” or “cost reduction.” Those labels do not answer the risk question. The buyer should assess the change against the failure modes that matter for the product: media performance, pressure drop, dust holding, sealing, structural strength, chemical compatibility, cleanliness, fit, marking and traceability.

A structured risk assessment can use severity, occurrence and detection, similar to the logic of failure mode and effects analysis. The buyer should be careful with a single risk-priority number: a high severity can be unacceptable even when occurrence and detection scores make the number look moderate. The assessment should identify which failure modes could be introduced, which controls would detect them and whether the existing validation remains valid.

If the change affects a characteristic that was approved during first article inspection, the buyer should require evidence against that characteristic. If it affects a characteristic that was never measured, the change may expose a gap in the original validation. That is a reason to expand the assessment, not a reason to call the change minor.

## 6. Use a Requalification Matrix to Decide the Evidence

A requalification matrix prevents two opposite errors: re-testing everything for a trivial change, and testing nothing because the supplier says the change is equivalent. The matrix should connect the change type to the required evidence, approval level and first-batch controls.

| Change type | Typical risk | Minimum evidence | Approval and release |
|---|---|---|---|
| Packaging or label content | Identification, handling or regulatory marking | Artwork proof, label inspection, barcode check | Commercial and quality approval |
| Media or gasket material | Performance, compatibility, durability | Material identity, performance comparison, first-article update | Technical and quality approval before shipment |
| Adhesive or bonding process | Seal integrity, strength, cure | Process validation, bond or leak checks, first-batch data | Technical approval and enhanced release |
| Pleat or geometry change | Pressure drop, area, fit, dust loading | Dimensional and performance comparison, FAI update | Technical approval and performance verification |
| New production line or site | Process control, traceability, capability | Site audit or line validation, process comparison, first-batch data | Quality approval and possibly a new audit |
| Sub-tier material supplier | Material identity, consistency | Approved-material evidence, incoming controls, change flow-down | Technical approval and incoming verification |
| Test method or instrument | Comparability of evidence, decision rule | Method validation, correlation data, uncertainty review | Quality approval before use |
| Traceability or labelling system | Containment, recall, record integrity | System validation, sample records, label verification | Quality approval and first-batch check |

The matrix is a starting point. The buyer should define the actual evidence for the product and risk, and should require a new approval when the change is significant enough to invalidate the original first article.

## 7. Do Not Accept “Equivalent” Without a Basis

Material equivalence is often asserted on the basis of a similar datasheet or a supplier's statement. A datasheet may show the same nominal grade while the actual material differs in basis weight, fibre composition, binder, pore structure or handling characteristics. A change that preserves one headline property can alter pressure drop, dust holding, tensile strength or compatibility with the adhesive.

The buyer should require the supplier to define the characteristic that matters and provide evidence for it. Where the change is a substitution, the assessment should compare the current and proposed material against the approved specification and the relevant performance characteristics. The comparison should use the same test method and conditions where possible; otherwise the buyer needs a bridging justification.

The [filter sample validation guide](/knowledge/filter-sample-validation/) describes how to verify material and construction with a production-representative sample. The change process should use that logic even when the supplier does not want to repeat a full sample programme. A short, targeted requalification can be enough if the failure modes and evidence are clear.

## 8. Control Site, Line, Equipment and Sub-Tier Moves

A change in site or line can be as consequential as a material change. Different equipment, operators, utilities, environmental conditions and sub-suppliers can alter the result even when the drawing is unchanged. The buyer should require a process comparison, a line or site validation and first-batch data before release.

A sub-tier change is easy to miss because the buyer may only see the finished filter. The supplier should identify which purchased materials or services are critical, require its own suppliers to notify changes and flow down the buyer's approval requirements where appropriate. If a media supplier changes a production site, the filter supplier's certificate of conformity may still be valid while the material is not.

The [factory audit guide](/knowledge/filter-factory-audit/) shows how to test sub-tier control on site. The audit should sample actual changes, not only the procedure, and follow the affected product to the customer notification record.

## 9. Link Change Approval to Production Release

An approved change should create a new production state. The approved configuration, control plan, inspection criteria, labels and training records should be updated. The first production batch after the change should be identified and may require enhanced inspection or retained samples. The buyer should not have to infer from a shipment which units were made before or after the change.

The release record should show that the approved change was implemented. If the supplier cannot distinguish pre-change and post-change inventory, the buyer may receive a mixed shipment without knowing which product has which status. This is especially important when the change affects performance but not appearance.

The [production control guide](/knowledge/filter-production-control/) explains how to set first-batch and routine release checks. The change approval should specify which of those checks are mandatory, how many batches are affected and when the process returns to normal monitoring.

## 10. Design Detection for Changes the Supplier Does Not Report

No system can rely solely on disclosure. The buyer should include checks that make an unannounced change more likely to be detected: incoming material identity, comparison with retained samples, dimensional checks, label and packaging review, periodic performance tests and supplier record audits. The checks should target characteristics that a substitution would change.

Retained samples are useful only if they are stored under controlled conditions, identified by lot and protected from damage. A visual comparison can detect a colour or construction change but not a subtle media substitution. A performance check can detect a change but may require the right method and conditions. The buyer should not treat any single check as complete protection.

Supplier data can also reveal change. A sudden shift in material supplier, process yield, defect profile or test result should prompt a question. The buyer should review the supplier's change log and approved-material list during routine reviews, not only during an audit.

## 11. Estimate the Exposure of an Unapproved Change

When a change is discovered, the buyer needs to estimate how much product may be affected. A simple expected-exposure calculation helps frame the response:

$$E=Q\times p$$

where $E$ is the expected number of affected units, $Q$ is the quantity produced under the changed condition and $p$ is the estimated failure probability for the relevant failure mode. The estimate is only as good as the evidence behind $p$; it is not a substitute for containment. If the failure probability is uncertain, the buyer should use a conservative range and inspect or test enough product to narrow it.

The exposure calculation should include inventory in the supplier's warehouse, in transit, at the buyer and at customers. It should also include service or replacement stock that may have been produced under the change. A change that affects only a small production run can still have a wide exposure if the affected units were distributed across several markets.

## 12. Watch for the Common Red Flags

Red flags include a supplier that cannot list the approved material or sub-supplier, a change log with no customer approvals, a certificate that is newer than the production lot, a datasheet that does not match the material certificate, and an explanation that a change is “the same” because the part number did not change. These are not proof of an intentional concealment, but they are reasons to hold release until the evidence is clear.

Another red flag is a buyer organisation that has no controlled approved configuration. If purchasing, engineering, quality and incoming inspection hold different revisions, the supplier may receive conflicting instructions. Change control begins inside the buyer's own team. The approved configuration should be the single reference for the purchase order, the supplier's process and the receiving check.

## 13. Use Retained Samples and Batch Data to Test Change Claims

Retained samples can support change verification when they are stored under controlled conditions, identified by lot and protected from light, moisture and handling damage. The buyer can compare construction, dimensions and performance between the retained sample and the changed product, provided the comparison uses the same method and conditions.

Retained samples are not a complete archive. They can change over time, they may not represent the full production spread and a small sample can miss a defect. They are most useful when combined with batch data from before and after the change. A sustained shift in mean, spread, first-pass yield or defect profile is evidence that the change affected the process, even if the sample passes a single test.

The buyer should define which samples are retained for which change type and who may release them for comparison. A sample selected by the supplier after the change is weaker evidence than a sample retained during the original approval. The change record should state how the comparison sample was chosen.

## 14. What this page does not claim.

This page is a change-control framework, not legal advice or a product-approval standard, and it does not establish that any supplier, material substitution or requalification plan is acceptable for a particular application. It does not guarantee that a supplier will disclose every change or that a sample test will detect every difference. The buyer remains responsible for defining the approved configuration, assessing the actual risk and verifying the affected product before release.
