An RFQ is not a screening tool. It is a request for a commercial offer against a defined requirement, and it becomes more useful when the supplier has already shown that it can plausibly meet that requirement. Sending a full drawing, target volume and packaging brief to every company that responds to a search result creates unnecessary disclosure, wastes engineering time and makes the eventual comparison harder to interpret.

Screening is the step before that. It reduces the supplier pool using evidence that is cheap to obtain and material to the decision: legal identity, production site, process fit, relevant quality-system scope, export readiness and any obvious risk that needs clarification first.

This guide sits inside the wider [filter supplier selection and risk-control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/). It is deliberately not a scorecard of everything a supplier might do well. A screen is a short, auditable decision about whether to invest in the next stage. Its most valuable outcome is often a documented reason not to proceed.

## 1. Screening Is a Knock-Out Gate, Not a Ranking

A ranking compares suppliers that have already met a minimum evidence threshold. A screen decides whether they have reached that threshold at all. Mixing the two produces a familiar problem: a supplier with a strong website, persuasive sales language and a low indicative price can outrank one with a narrower but better-documented process.

The screen should therefore use three outcomes. Proceed means the supplier is eligible for the defined RFQ and the open questions can be carried into technical review. Hold means a material fact is missing or inconsistent and must be resolved before disclosure or quotation. Reject means the supplier cannot meet a basic requirement, such as making the product from the proposed site, holding a required legal status, or accepting the buyer's inspection and change-control conditions.

The outcome should identify the product family and site. A supplier can be approved for one commodity and rejected for another without either decision being inconsistent. The buyer should record the screen date, reviewer, sources checked and the evidence still outstanding. A screenshot of a certificate is not a substitute for a record of what was verified.

The screen also has a disclosure boundary. Detailed drawings, tooling files, customer part numbers and volume forecasts should be released only after confidentiality and the commercial route are understood. A pre-RFQ screen can use representative dimensions, product type and a target performance envelope. It does not need the buyer's full design file to establish basic fit.

## 2. Begin With the Purchase, Not the Supplier Website

The first input to a screen is the purchase itself. What is being bought, for which application, under which specification, at what expected recurrence, and through which delivery route? A one-off replacement order, a recurring private-label programme and a safety-related production input justify different levels of evidence. The supplier's marketing cannot define the risk tier.

Write a short purchase profile before opening supplier websites. The profile should include the filter family, the critical interface, the performance or test requirements, the expected annual or campaign volume, the destination market, the required label and packaging, and any customer-specific documentation. It should also state which failure would be unacceptable. For a simple panel filter, the critical issue may be dimensional fit and media integrity. For a high-efficiency product, it may be local leak integrity and test traceability.

The purchase profile prevents the screen from becoming a comparison of unrelated companies. A large HVAC filter manufacturer may be a poor fit for a small automotive cabin-filter programme, while a specialist automotive supplier may be unable to support a cleanroom product. The question is not which company looks strongest in general, but which company is credible for this purchase.

## 3. Verify Legal Identity and the Supplier's Role

The legal name, registration identifier and registered address should be checked before a quotation is treated as a commitment. In China, the business licence is a basic identity document and the unified social credit code identifies the entity. The buyer should check that the name on the quotation, bank account, business licence, quality certificate and proposed contract is consistent, or that any difference is explained by a documented relationship.

A manufacturer, a trading company and an export agent play different roles. None is automatically unacceptable. A trading company may provide useful consolidation, export documentation or quality coordination. The risk is that the buyer assumes it is dealing with the production site when it is not. The screen should identify the legal seller, the manufacturing entity, the production site and the party responsible for quality release.

The supplier should explain the relationship between those parties. If the seller is a trading company, the buyer needs to know whether the manufacturer is named in the quality agreement, whether the buyer can audit the production site, who controls changes and who handles claims. If the seller is a manufacturer, the buyer should still confirm that the proposed site is within the seller's control and certification scope.

Business-licence checks should not stop at the document image. Compare the registered scope, address and entity type with the proposed purchase, and use the verified legal name in the contract. A translated or abbreviated website name is not enough to resolve a mismatch.

## 4. Establish Which Site and Process Will Make the Product

Supplier screening often fails at the site question because the answer is treated as obvious. It is not. A company may have several buildings, a separate finishing operation, a warehouse that performs labelling, or a subcontractor for a process it does not own. The buyer needs to know which of those locations contributes to the product and which is merely commercial.

A useful answer includes a simple process map: where media or components are received, where the filter is formed or assembled, where sealing or curing occurs, where testing and packaging take place, and where finished goods are stored. The buyer does not need a plant layout at screening, but it does need to know whether all critical steps occur at the proposed site or move between sites.

If a critical process is outsourced, the screen should flag it. Outsourcing is not inherently bad, but it changes the audit scope, traceability chain and change obligations. The buyer may need access to the subcontractor, confirmation that the subcontractor's work is included in the quality system, and a clear description of responsibility when a defect originates there. A supplier that cannot name the site of a critical process is not ready for a production RFQ.

## 5. Check Product and Process Fit, Not Catalogue Breadth

A catalogue can show that a supplier makes filters. It does not show that it makes the buyer's filter with the required controls. Product fit should be checked against the proposed construction, dimensions, materials, performance class and test method. Process fit should be checked against the critical manufacturing steps and the supplier's evidence of controlling them.

For a relatively simple product, the relevant evidence may be dimensional capability, media handling, pleating equipment, sealing and inspection. For a product where performance is sensitive to media area, pleat geometry or airflow, the buyer should ask how the supplier verifies the relationship between design and finished performance. The [air filter performance framework](/knowledge/air-filter-performance/) is useful here because it separates headline efficiency from pressure drop, dust loading and service behaviour.

Ask for examples of similar products without treating a catalogue photograph as proof that the supplier produces them. A useful question is not “Can you make this?” but “Which line, tooling and test method would you use, and what evidence exists that this combination has produced a conforming product?” The answer should be specific enough to compare with the purchase profile.

Where the supplier cannot yet answer, the buyer should distinguish a commercial gap from a capability gap. A supplier may need to develop tooling or qualify a new material, which can be a legitimate project. It should not be described as a standard supply route until the development and first-article plan are defined. Good screening exposes that difference early.

## 6. Read Quality-System Evidence by Scope and Site

Quality-system certificates are useful evidence of a management structure, not proof that a particular filter conforms. ISO 9001 covers quality management system requirements and can apply across many industries. IATF 16949 is the automotive quality management system framework for organisations within its defined automotive scope. ISO 13485 applies to medical-device quality management where that regulatory and product context applies. The buyer should ask what the certificate covers, which legal entity and site hold it, and whether the product being purchased falls within that scope.

A certificate image should be checked for the issuing body, number, standard and edition, scope, site, dates and conditions. A supplier may have a certificate for a different site, product family or shortened scope, or one that has expired but remains online. Verify through the certification body's published route rather than relying only on the PDF.

The screen should record the certificate as evidence with a limitation. A current ISO 9001 certificate to the right site supports the existence of a controlled management system. It does not establish the capability of a particular process, the validity of a test method or the accuracy of a product claim. Those questions are addressed by audit, first-article and test evidence.

The following table is a screening aid, not a substitute for the certificate and its verification.

| Evidence | What it can support at screening | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Business licence | Legal identity, registered scope and address | Production capability at the proposed site |
| ISO 9001 certificate | A certified quality management system within its stated scope | Product conformity or every process control |
| IATF 16949 certificate | Automotive quality-system certification within its stated scope | Applicability to a product or customer outside that scope |
| ISO 13485 certificate | Medical-device quality management within its stated scope | Regulatory approval of a particular device or filter |
| ISO 14644-related evidence | Cleanroom classification or testing capability where relevant | Classification or integrity of every filter supplied |
| Customer references | Existence of a supply relationship, if verifiable | Performance on the buyer's specification |

## 7. Ask for Production Evidence, Not Only Presentation

A supplier that can provide a coherent process description, equipment list, control plan outline and recent production record sample is easier to assess than one that provides only a brochure. The screen does not need to verify the entire factory. It needs to see whether evidence exists and whether the supplier can explain it without contradictions.

Useful evidence at this stage includes the type of production lines used for the proposed product, the in-house versus outsourced steps, the routine release-test approach, the calibration system, the handling of nonconforming goods and the traceability unit. The supplier should also be able to explain how a production lot is defined and how a finished filter is linked to its material and process records. If that explanation is vague, the later audit has a clear focus.

The buyer should be cautious with documents that are obviously generic. A control plan that names no product characteristic, a test report that cannot identify the tested object or a certificate of conformity that repeats the specification without results adds little. The [filter quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) and [filter sample validation](/knowledge/filter-sample-validation/) guides show how these evidence expectations become more specific after screening.

## 8. Assess Export and Documentation Readiness

The supplier's ability to deliver is partly a documentation capability. The buyer should confirm whether the supplier or its nominated agent can issue a consistent commercial invoice, packing list, transport document, certificate of origin where applicable, customs declaration and any product-specific declaration required by the destination. The exact document set depends on the product, route and import market.

The screen should check that the supplier can use the intended Incoterms 2020 rule and named place correctly. Incoterms allocate cost, risk and delivery obligations, but they do not replace the sales contract, payment terms or product compliance obligations. A supplier that quotes “FOB” without a port or “DDP” without understanding the import requirements has not yet demonstrated a workable delivery arrangement.

Export experience should be tested with questions rather than inferred from a website. Which markets have been served? Who prepares the customs documents? How are labelling and language requirements handled? What happens when a shipment is held for inspection? These questions reveal whether the commercial route is routine or improvised. The answers should be consistent with the documents the supplier can show.

The screen should also flag product-regulation claims. A CE or UKCA mark is not automatically required for every filter. Applicability depends on the product, its intended use and the market's legislation. A supplier that applies a mark without being able to explain the applicable route and supporting documents has created a risk rather than removed one.

## 9. Review Capacity and Commercial Fit Before Disclosure

Screening does not require a capacity study, but it should remove obvious mismatches. The buyer should know whether the proposed order is a routine share of the supplier's output or a campaign that would consume a large part of it. The supplier should be able to describe its normal planning horizon, its peak season, its shift pattern and how it handles a quality hold or equipment breakdown.

Questions about minimum order quantity and lead time are legitimate, but they should be asked against the purchase profile rather than as abstract benchmarks. The [MOQ, lead time and pricing guide](/guides/filter-moq-lead-time/) explains why those figures depend on product, tooling, packaging and scheduling. A supplier that supplies a single indicative number without asking about the specification should be asked to qualify it.

The screen should also check payment expectations at a high level. A request for a deposit is not a red flag by itself. A request for payment to a personal account, a third-party account or an entity different from the contracting seller is a material issue that must be resolved before an order. The commercial risk guide linked from the pillar explains how payment and delivery terms should connect to identifiable milestones.

When the purchase is ready for a formal quotation, the buyer can issue it through the [RFQ route](/rfq/). The RFQ should carry the screened supplier list, the purchase profile, the required documents and the buyer's conditions. Screening is what makes that RFQ a focused request rather than a broadcast.

## 10. Use Capability Data Carefully

A supplier may offer process capability data at the screening stage. The data can be useful, but only if its method is clear. A simple capability index for a two-sided characteristic is:

$$C_{pk}=\min\left(\frac{USL-\mu}{3\sigma},\frac{\mu-LSL}{3\sigma}\right)$$

where $USL$ and $LSL$ are the upper and lower specification limits, $\mu$ is the process mean and $\sigma$ is the process standard deviation. The index compares the process spread and centring with the specification width. It does not rescue a specification that is wrong for the application, a measurement system that is inadequate or a data set that was selected from only the best production period.

At screening, the buyer should ask what characteristic the index describes, how many samples and subgroups were used, when the data were collected, whether the process was in statistical control and whether the measurement system was assessed. A high index from an unknown measurement system is weaker than a modest index from a well-described study. The buyer should also understand that a capability index is not a product release criterion unless the purchase specification makes it one.

For attribute data, a first-pass yield may be more intuitive:

$$FPY=\frac{\text{units conforming on first pass}}{\text{units started}}$$

A supplier that reworks a high proportion of output can appear to have a good final yield while consuming extra labour, time and handling risk. The screen should ask separately about first-pass yield, rework and scrap.

## 11. Treat Inconsistency as a Red Flag

The most useful screening signal is often inconsistency. The stated production address may differ from the certificate; the quotation and bank details may name different companies; a product may be listed under a standard the supplier cannot explain. Each issue may have an innocent explanation, but the buyer should not resolve it by assumption.

Other red flags include refusal to identify the production site, reluctance to explain the relationship with a trading company, a certificate image with unreadable scope, a request to pay a different entity, a claim of unlimited customisation without an engineering review, and an inability to describe how a change to media or gasket material would be approved. None of these proves misconduct. Each is a reason to hold the supplier until the evidence is clear.

An automatic reject is appropriate when the supplier cannot meet a basic legal or control requirement and is unwilling to accept the buyer's verification terms. A hold is appropriate when the missing fact can be resolved. A proceed decision is appropriate when the evidence is sufficient for the next stage and the remaining questions are specific.

## 12. What this page does not claim.

This page describes a pre-RFQ screening framework; it is not legal, financial or product-certification advice and does not guarantee that a supplier will perform as represented. It does not approve any named supplier, certificate or test result, and a complete screen does not replace a site audit, first-article inspection or production verification. The buyer remains responsible for confirming the actual legal entity, site, product, applicable standards and contractual terms for each purchase.
