A claim is rarely won or lost only on the quality of the product. It is also decided by the quality of the record. Can the buyer identify the affected lot? Is the test report linked to the product that failed? Does the certificate cover the site and product? Is the invoice consistent with the shipment? Did the buyer preserve the evidence and notify the supplier within the contractual period? These questions determine whether a technical problem becomes a resolvable claim or an expensive argument.

Documentation risk sits at the end of the [supplier selection and risk-control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/), but it should be designed at the beginning. The quality agreement, the purchase order, the Incoterm, the label and the batch record all create the evidence that will be used later. A document created after a failure is often weaker than one created during normal supply.

This guide explains what common documents can and cannot establish, how to review an export documentation chain, and how to prepare a claim response. It is not legal advice. The practical objective is to make the evidence chain complete enough that the technical facts can be assessed without unnecessary ambiguity.

## 1. Claims Risk Begins Before the Shipment

A buyer often starts thinking about claims when a defect is found. By then, the most useful evidence may already be missing. The shipment may have been mixed with another lot, the packaging may have been discarded, the certificate may not identify the product, or the supplier may have changed the material without any record in the buyer's file.

The buyer should specify the evidence chain before the order is placed. At a minimum, the purchase record should identify the product, specification revision, quantity, lot or batch marking, packaging, destination and required documents. The supplier should confirm how the goods will be identified and which records will accompany the shipment. The buyer should also know which records it must retain.

The documentation chain should follow the physical chain. The carton label should connect to the lot record, the lot record to the production and material records, and the shipment documents to the purchase order and invoice. If those connections exist only in separate spreadsheets, the supplier may still be able to reconstruct them, but the buyer should test that ability before a dispute.

## 2. Read Certificates as Scoped Claims

Certificates are often treated as general proof of quality. They are better understood as statements issued within a scope and against a defined standard. ISO 9001 certifies a quality management system within its scope. IATF 16949 certifies an automotive quality management system for eligible organisations within its stated scope. ISO 13485 certifies a medical-device quality management system where that framework applies. None of these certificates is a product-specific performance certificate for every filter the supplier makes.

The buyer should check the legal entity, site, scope, standard and edition, certificate number, issuing body, dates and any conditions. A certificate for a different site or product family may be genuine and still irrelevant. A certificate that has expired, is suspended or has a scope that excludes the purchased product should not be accepted as current evidence. Verification through the issuing body is more reliable than a PDF attached to an email.

A supplier may also provide a certificate of conformity or a certificate of analysis for a lot. These documents can be useful, but they are only as good as the underlying test and the authority of the signer. A certificate of conformity that repeats the specification without data may be a declaration, not evidence of conformity. The [quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) should define what the certificate must contain, who may sign it and what records support it.

## 3. Establish Whether CE or UKCA Applies

CE and UKCA markings are not universal filter labels. Whether a marking is required depends on the product, its intended use and the legislation of the market in which it is placed. A filter incorporated into a machine may be assessed as part of the machine's conformity, while a filter sold as a standalone product or a medical device may follow a different route. The buyer should identify the applicable regulation before accepting a mark as evidence.

Where the legislation applies, the supplier should be able to identify the conformity assessment route, the applicable designated or harmonised standards, the technical file or declaration, and the role of any notified body or approved body. A declaration of conformity is not a substitute for the technical evidence behind it. A logo on a label is not evidence that the correct route was followed.

The importer or distributor may have its own obligations under the applicable market rules. The buyer should not assume that the Chinese supplier has taken responsibility for every import, labelling or market-surveillance requirement. The contract, the importer of record and the market-access plan should make those responsibilities explicit.

## 4. Distinguish Test Reports, Certificates and Declarations

A test report records a result for a stated test object under stated conditions and method. A certificate of analysis usually reports measured characteristics for a material or lot. A certificate of conformity is a declaration that specified requirements are met. A declaration of conformity is a formal statement under an applicable conformity-assessment regime. These documents serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable.

The buyer should read the test report against the claim being made. Does it identify the product or material, the sample, the standard and edition, the test conditions, the results, the uncertainty and the decision rule? Does it show that the laboratory was competent for the test? Is the report for the same revision and construction as the product being purchased? The [filter test report guide](/knowledge/read-filter-test-report/) provides a detailed review sequence.

For a lot claim, the buyer should connect the report to the lot. A report dated before the production of the lot, a report for a prototype that differs from the production configuration or a report with no sample identity is weaker evidence. The [first article inspection guide](/knowledge/filter-first-article-inspection/) explains how to make that connection at approval; the same logic applies to routine lots.

## 5. Build a Traceability Chain, Not a Folder of Files

Traceability is the ability to follow a product forward and backward through the supply chain. For a filter, the chain may include the finished-goods lot, production date and line, material lots, incoming inspection, process settings, release tests, packaging and shipment. The exact chain depends on the product and the risk.

The buyer should test both directions. From a delivered carton, can the supplier identify the production lot, the material lots and the release record? From a material lot, can the supplier identify the affected finished goods and shipments? A traceability system that works only in one direction is incomplete for containment.

Traceability coverage can be expressed as a simple proportion for a sample of units or lots:

$$TC=\frac{\text{units with a complete traceable record}}{\text{units sampled}}$$

The result describes the sample, not the entire population. A supplier that can trace every sampled unit has stronger evidence than one that can trace only the units selected by the buyer in advance. The buyer should record how the sample was selected and what record was required to count as complete.

## 6. Check Export Documents for Consistency

The export document set depends on the product, route, destination and transaction. It may include a commercial invoice, packing list, sales contract, transport document, certificate of origin where applicable, customs declaration, product labels, test reports or certificates and any declaration required by the destination. Dangerous-goods or other special documents are needed only where the product or route requires them; a filter should not be assumed to fall into a category without checking.

The documents should agree with one another. The invoice description, quantity and value should match the purchase order and packing list. The seller and buyer names should match the contract and the bank details. The transport document should identify the correct route and consignee. The origin certificate, where used, should be the correct type for the applicable trade arrangement. The product label should match the product and destination requirements.

In China, export declarations are normally made by a registered exporter or a customs broker under the applicable procedures. The buyer should know who the exporter of record is, who controls the customs declaration and which party is responsible for any inspection or quarantine requirement. Whether a particular filter requires statutory inspection depends on its classification and current rules; the buyer should verify that for the actual product and destination rather than assume from a generic checklist.

The [RFQ route](/rfq/) can be used to request the intended document set and delivery terms before the order is placed. The supplier's response should be specific enough to compare with the import plan.

## 7. Connect Incoterms to the Claim Boundary

The Incoterms rule determines when risk passes from seller to buyer for the movement of goods, subject to the sales contract. It does not determine whether the product conforms to the specification. A buyer that receives damaged goods after the risk-transfer point may have a transport insurance claim rather than a supplier quality claim, depending on the facts and the contract.

The buyer should know the named place and the point at which risk passes. If the supplier arranges insurance under CIF, the buyer should know the cover and the beneficiary. If the buyer arranges carriage under FCA or another rule, the buyer should ensure that the carrier and insurance align with the route. The claim process should start with the correct party and the correct evidence: transport damage, packaging failure, customs hold, quantity shortage and product nonconformity are different events.

The delivery term should also be consistent with the inspection plan. If the buyer intends to inspect before shipment, the contract should provide access and a hold point before the goods leave the supplier's control. If inspection happens after arrival, the buyer should preserve the packaging and notify the supplier within the agreed period.

## 8. Prepare the Claim Response Before the Claim

A claim procedure should state who is notified, how quickly, with what evidence and what the supplier must do. The buyer should preserve the affected product, packaging, labels, shipping documents, photographs, test data and correspondence. It should quarantine the lot and prevent further distribution where safety or regulatory risk justifies it.

The supplier should provide a containment plan, an investigation and a corrective action. The buyer should not accept a replacement shipment as the whole response if the cause is unknown and the same condition may affect other lots. The [batch failure and corrective action guide](/knowledge/filter-batch-failure-capa/) explains how to connect containment, root cause and effectiveness.

The claim record should identify the affected quantity, the failure mode, the evidence, the contractual requirement and the proposed remedy. It should also identify the party responsible for freight, inspection, sorting, replacement and customer communication. The exact allocation depends on the contract and applicable law, but it should not be left to informal negotiation after the product is gone.

## 9. Estimate Claim Exposure With Transparent Assumptions

The cost of a claim is not limited to the invoice value. A simple claim-cost expression is:

$$C_{\text{claim}}=C_{\text{replacement}}+C_{\text{freight}}+C_{\text{sorting}}+C_{\text{downtime}}+C_{\text{administration}}$$

Each term should be estimated from the actual situation. Replacement cost is the product and freight needed to restore supply. Sorting cost includes inspection, handling and disposal. Downtime may include production interruption, expediting or customer penalties. Administration includes investigation, legal and quality effort. The expression makes the cost structure visible; it does not convert an uncertain event into a precise forecast.

The number of affected units can be estimated from the shipment quantity and the suspected defect prevalence:

$$E_{\text{affected}}=Q\times p$$

where $Q$ is the quantity in the affected scope and $p$ is the estimated defect rate. If $p$ is unknown, the buyer should use a range and inspect or test enough product to narrow it. The estimate is useful for planning containment and commercial recovery, not for deciding whether a defect exists.

## 10. Review Documents With a Consistent Matrix

Document review is easier when the buyer uses the same matrix for every shipment. The matrix should show the document, the purpose, the party that issues it, the check performed and the evidence retained. A missing document may be a commercial issue, a customs issue or a quality issue; the matrix should make the consequence visible.

| Document | Purpose | Check | Failure consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Defines the transaction and customs value | Buyer, seller, quantity, value and description consistency | Payment, customs or claim mismatch |
| Packing list | Links cartons to quantity and shipment | Carton count, weights, marks and lot identification | Shortage or traceability gap |
| Transport document | Records carriage and delivery | Consignee, route, port or place and condition | Wrong party or route for a claim |
| Certificate of origin | Supports origin treatment where applicable | Type, issuer, goods and destination | Preferential treatment or clearance issue |
| Test report | Supports a product or lot claim | Sample identity, method, conditions and result | Unsupported conformity claim |
| Certificate of conformity or analysis | States compliance or measured values | Signer, lot, specification and supporting data | Weak release or claim evidence |
| Management-system certificate | Supports a system scope | Entity, site, scope, dates and verification | Irrelevant or expired assurance |
| Label and packaging record | Identifies product and protects it | Product, lot, language, warnings and condition | Misidentification or damage |
| Customs declaration | Records export clearance | Exporter, goods description, value and status | Clearance delay or documentation gap |

## 11. Retain Records for the Life of the Exposure

The buyer should define a retention period based on the product, customer requirements, regulatory obligations, warranty period and contract. The record should be retrievable by lot and by shipment, not only by supplier or invoice. Electronic records should have access controls, backups and a readable format. A scanned image that cannot be searched or linked is better than nothing, but it is not a complete system.

The supplier's retention obligation should be in the quality agreement. The buyer should confirm that the supplier will retain batch, material, test, change and complaint records for the agreed period and will make them available on request or during an audit. If the supplier uses a subcontractor, the retention obligation should flow down.

The buyer should also retain the approved configuration and the correspondence that changed it. A test report without the specification it was assessed against is difficult to interpret. A certificate without the purchase order and lot identity may not connect to the delivered product.

## 12. Use Third-Party Inspection as an Optional Service

Independent inspection firms such as SGS, BV or TÜV may be contracted to perform a defined inspection, audit or test. Such a service can add useful evidence when the scope, sampling plan, method and reporting requirements are clear. It is not a substitute for the buyer's specification, and it does not make the inspection firm a party to the supply contract unless the parties agree otherwise.

The buyer should confirm the firm's scope, the inspector's competence for the product, the inspection criteria, the sample size, the acceptance rule and the deliverable. An inspection report that says “no obvious defect” without the criteria and sample is difficult to use in a claim. The buyer should also avoid implying that a named firm is affiliated with the supplier or the site unless that relationship is documented.

The report should be retained with the shipment and the purchase record, not filed only in the supplier's quality folder. A later claim may need the inspection plan, the sample identity, the inspector's observations and the photographs, not just the final pass statement.

## The practical position

The practical position is that a claim file should be built around the shipment, not the supplier's certificate library. We would accept a document only when its product, site, lot and test conditions can be mapped to the goods; otherwise it is context, not evidence. Buyers should rehearse a claim before placing the order by asking which record would prove identity, condition and responsibility if the delivery failed. If that question cannot be answered, the documentation clause is decorative. A supplier that cannot support a dry run is unlikely to reconstruct the file during a dispute.

## 13. What this page does not claim.

This page is a documentation and claims-planning framework, not legal, customs, tax or regulatory advice and not a substitute for advice on the applicable market rules. It does not establish that any certificate, test report, declaration, export document or claim is valid or sufficient for a particular product or destination. The buyer remains responsible for verifying the actual documents, the applicable requirements and the contractual rights with qualified advisers.
