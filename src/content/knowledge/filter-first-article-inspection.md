A first article inspection is often confused with sample approval. A supplier sends a well-made sample, the buyer checks the dimensions and appearance, and the product is treated as approved. That sequence leaves the most important question unanswered: was the sample made with the same tooling, materials, process, operators and production conditions that will be used for the order?

First article inspection is a production approval event. It examines whether the defined product can be manufactured to the specification and whether the evidence is complete enough to release the process. It is one gate in the [supplier selection and control framework](/knowledge/filter-supplier-selection/), downstream of screening and audit. The broader commercial sequence from RFQ to first article is described in the [OEM manufacturing guide](/guides/oem-procurement-process/), but the approval logic here is technical.

The buyer should treat the first article as an argument that must be reconstructed. What was made, by which process, from which materials, against which criteria, measured how, and with what result? If the argument has a missing link, the approval is weaker than it appears.

## 1. Separate Sample, First Article and Production Lot Approval

A sample is a physical object supplied for evaluation. A first article is a sample or set of samples with defined provenance and a documented manufacturing context. A production lot approval is a release decision for a defined quantity produced under the approved process.

These are separate claims. A sample can show that a design is possible. A first article can show that the production process can make the design under controlled conditions. A production lot approval can show that a particular lot was made within the approved state and released against the agreed criteria. Passing one does not automatically prove the next.

The confusion is especially costly for filters because appearance and clean-state dimensions can look correct while media grade, effective area, adhesive, gasket compression or leak integrity differ. Those characteristics may determine field performance. The [filter sample validation guide](/knowledge/filter-sample-validation/) describes the deeper checks that support a production-representative sample.

First article inspection also overlaps with, but is not identical to, the Production Part Approval Process used in automotive supply chains under the AIAG PPAP framework. PPAP is a customer-defined approval package and process; FAI is one part of the evidence in some programmes. The applicable customer requirements determine what is required. A buyer should not assume that a document called “FAI” satisfies a PPAP requirement or vice versa.

## 2. Define the Approval Scope Before Producing the Article

The first article should be produced against an approved specification. That sounds obvious, but programmes often begin with a drawing, a sample and several email clarifications. The first-article report then tests what the supplier understood, not what the buyer intended.

Before production, the buyer should lock the revision of the drawing, specification and test method. It should define the product configuration, the tooling and production line, the material grades, the packaging and the acceptance criteria. It should also define which deviations are permitted to be requested, which are not, and who has authority to approve them.

The scope should state which sites, lines and shifts are covered. If the product will later move to another line or be made by a subcontractor, that change should trigger a new approval or a defined extension. A first article made on a pilot line may be useful, but it does not automatically approve a high-volume line.

The buyer should also agree the sample quantity and identity before production. It is easier for the supplier to retain and label samples during the run than to select them afterward. The record should show how the article was drawn from the production stream and whether it represents the first stable output or a specially prepared unit.

## 3. Turn the Specification Into Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria should be written so that two competent reviewers would reach the same decision. “Good finish,” “suitable media” and “proper seal” are not criteria. The record should identify the characteristic, the method, the sample, the limits and the decision rule.

For a filter, the criteria commonly cover dimensions and interfaces, material and construction, performance under stated test conditions, workmanship, labelling, packaging and documents. Not every criterion needs a laboratory test. Some can be verified by drawing, certificate, visual inspection or fit trial. The important point is that each criterion has a defined evidence path.

The acceptance criteria should reflect the function of the filter, not only the drawing. A dimensional tolerance may have little meaning without the mating housing or gasket condition. A pressure-drop limit may be meaningless without the airflow at which it applies. The [air filter performance framework](/knowledge/air-filter-performance/) explains how to keep the test conditions attached to the performance claim.

Where the buyer specifies a test standard, the first-article record should identify the edition, part and test conditions actually used. A result from a different method is not necessarily comparable. The [filter test report reading guide](/knowledge/read-filter-test-report/) sets out the questions that make a result interpretable.

## 4. Verify the Provenance of the Article

The provenance section is the difference between a sample and a first article. It should identify the supplier and site, the production date and lot, the line and shift, the tooling and equipment, the material lots, the process settings and the operators or work cells where relevant. It should also state any special preparation used for the article.

A production-representative article should come from a normal production run, or from a clearly defined pilot run that uses the production tooling and process. If the supplier polished the samples by hand, selected the best units or used a temporary tool, the report should say so. That does not make the article useless, but it changes what it proves.

The buyer can test provenance by asking for the associated production record. If the article's serial number or lot cannot be linked to the material and process record, the evidence is incomplete. The [factory audit guide](/knowledge/filter-factory-audit/) explains how to test that linkage on site; the first article applies the same logic to a specific approval.

## 5. Check Dimensions, Fit and the Sealing Interface

Dimensional inspection should cover the characteristics that affect installation, sealing and function: overall dimensions, mounting features, gasket position and compression, frame flatness, pleat or media envelope where relevant, and any interface specified by the drawing. Measuring only the outside dimensions can miss a gasket that sits too high, a frame that is twisted or a mounting feature that will not engage.

The measurement method should be appropriate to the characteristic. A caliper may be adequate for a stable external dimension and inadequate for a flexible gasket profile or a seal surface. The record should state the instrument, the datum, the sample location and the result. For flexible or compressible features, the buyer should define the measurement condition and whether the value represents free state or installed state.

Fit should be checked against the actual mating interface where possible. A drawing-based check can confirm nominal dimensions; a housing or fixture trial can reveal interference, poor sealing or assembly difficulty. The result should be recorded as a condition, not just a pass or fail, because a tight fit may become a field problem after temperature or humidity changes.

## 6. Verify Material Identity and Construction

Material verification should connect the finished article to the approved material specification. The report can include the supplier's material certificate, incoming inspection record, a laboratory identity check or a destructive examination of construction. The method should be sufficient for the risk; a certificate alone may not show that the material in the article matches the certificate.

Construction checks may include media grade and basis, pleat count or geometry, effective area, adhesive coverage, frame material, gasket compound and bonding condition. A destructive cross-section can reveal voids, incomplete adhesive or a material that looks correct from outside. The sample used for destructive examination should be identified and should not be the only unit available for performance testing.

The [filter quality agreement](/knowledge/filter-quality-agreement/) should define which material and construction records are required and how changes to them are approved. If the first article is approved on a material that is not the production material, the approval has a gap that should be closed before shipment.

## 7. Review Performance Evidence at the Intended Conditions

Performance evidence should match the purchase specification. Depending on the filter and application, it may include efficiency or classification, pressure drop, dust loading, flow or integrity testing. The report should identify the test standard and edition, the test object, the sample identity, the airflow or other operating conditions, the instrument or facility and the result.

The buyer should not accept a performance result whose conditions cannot be compared with the intended use. A result at a different airflow, a test on flat media rather than a finished element, or a classification with no sample provenance may be helpful background but does not approve the article. The [read-filter-test-report guide](/knowledge/read-filter-test-report/) provides a structured review.

If the first article passes performance but fails a dimensional or construction criterion, the buyer should not average the two into a general approval. Each critical requirement should have a decision. A concession on one characteristic may be acceptable if its consequence is understood and controlled; it should not be hidden inside a summary score.

## 8. State the Measurement Decision Rule

Measurements are not perfectly exact. The first-article record should say how measurement uncertainty is treated at the specification limits. A defensible decision rule can be written as:

$$x+U\leq USL$$

for an upper specification limit, where $x$ is the measured value and $U$ is the expanded uncertainty appropriate to the decision. The rule should be applied consistently, including when the measured value is inside the limit but the uncertainty band crosses it. The buyer and supplier should agree whether the basis is simple acceptance, shared risk or guard-banding.

The rule should not be invented only for the first article. If routine production uses a different acceptance approach, the first article may approve a condition that the release system cannot maintain. The measurement system, operators and environmental conditions should be as close as practicable to those used in production.

## 9. Do Not Treat FAI as an AQL Sampling Decision

First article inspection asks whether the process can produce a conforming article. Acceptance sampling asks whether a lot should be accepted under a defined sampling plan. They answer different questions and should not be merged into a single “passed” statement.

ISO 2859-1 provides an attribute sampling framework indexed by lot size, inspection level and AQL. The operating characteristic of a simple plan can be expressed with the hypergeometric acceptance probability:

$$P_a=\sum_{k=0}^{c}\frac{\binom{D}{k}\binom{N-D}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}$$

where $N$ is the lot size, $D$ the number of nonconforming units, $n$ the sample size and $c$ the acceptance number. The formula shows why an accepted sample does not prove a defect-free lot. A first article can be one unit and still be valuable for process approval; a routine lot may need a sampling plan for release. Neither is a substitute for the other.

## 10. Assemble a First Article Document Pack

The document pack should let a reviewer reconstruct the approval without interviewing the supplier. It should include the approved configuration, the article identity and provenance, the inspection results, the performance evidence, the deviations and the approval decision. The buyer should define which documents are originals, which may be copies and how long they are retained.

| Document | Purpose | Minimum trace | Common gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approved drawing and specification | Defines the configuration and limits | Revision and approval date | Superseded revision used |
| Material declarations and certificates | Shows approved material identity | Material lot to article lot | Certificate not linked to production |
| Process and tooling record | Shows how the article was made | Line, tool, settings, date | Sample prepared outside normal process |
| Dimensional report | Records fit and interface results | Characteristic, method, sample ID | Results without measurement method |
| Performance test report | Shows relevant filter performance | Test object, conditions, method | Test on unrepresentative sample |
| Workmanship and packaging record | Covers defects and shipping protection | Inspection criterion and result | Visual acceptance not defined |
| Deviation and concession record | Makes exceptions visible | Requirement, reason, approver | Verbal concession not documented |
| Approval and release record | States the decision and conditions | Reviewer, date, scope | Approval limited without expiry |

The document pack should be indexed. A folder of files with inconsistent names and no revision control is not a reliable approval record.

The buyer should verify the authenticity and relevance of certificates and reports included in the pack. A supplier may provide a genuine report for a different sample or an older revision. The review should compare the report's object, date and method with the article and the specification, and should resolve any unexplained difference before approving.

## 11. Manage Deviations and Conditional Approval

A supplier may propose a deviation because a material is unavailable, a tool needs adjustment or a measurement is close to a limit. The buyer should have a process for evaluating it. The request should identify the requirement, the proposed alternative, the affected samples or lots, the technical consequence and the proposed validation.

A conditional approval can be appropriate when the deviation does not affect a critical characteristic, or when the risk is understood and the condition is verifiable. Conditions should have an owner and an expiry. A conditional approval that is renewed indefinitely without evidence becomes a permanent unapproved configuration.

For a safety, regulatory or critical fit characteristic, the buyer should be especially cautious about accepting a deviation without independent technical review. The supplier's commercial urgency is not a reason to change the acceptance criterion. The decision should be recorded separately from the technical assessment.

## 12. Hand the Approval Back to Production Control

The first article is not complete when the report is signed. The approved configuration, process settings and acceptance criteria must be transferred to production control. The control plan should reflect the characteristics verified in the first article, and the release records should show that routine production uses the same methods.

The buyer should define the first production lot requirements. They may include a higher inspection frequency, a retained sample, confirmation that the approved material lots were used and a review of the first batch data before shipment. The [production control guide](/knowledge/filter-production-control/) develops the ongoing monitoring system.

Approval should also identify revalidation triggers: a change in material, process, tooling, site, sub-supplier, test method or significant capacity. Those triggers should return the product to a defined level of first-article evidence, not simply to a new sales sample.

## 13. Avoid the Common First Article Shortcuts

The most common shortcut is to approve the best-looking sample without knowing how it was made. Others include using a different material from the intended production material, testing a single dimension because it is easy to measure, accepting a certificate in place of inspection, and allowing a deviation through an email that no one links to the approved file.

Another shortcut is to treat the FAI report as a one-time customer deliverable rather than a living production reference. The approved configuration should be available to incoming inspection, purchasing, the supplier's quality team and the people handling changes. If the organization cannot retrieve the approved configuration when a batch is questioned, the approval is not operational.

A good first article does not guarantee a good supplier. It is one piece of evidence in a chain. Its value is that it converts a general claim of capability into a specific, traceable approval against the product the buyer intends to receive.

## The practical position

The practical position is that a first article without production provenance is just a good sample. We would not release serial supply until the tool, line, material lots and settings behind the article are identified and the first routine output is checked against them. A supplier that cannot reproduce the article's conditions has demonstrated craft, not capability. Conditional approval should expire unless the missing production evidence arrives. The buyer's protection lies in making the approval transferable to the line, not in preserving a prized sample on a shelf.

## 14. What this page does not claim.

This page is a first-article planning and review framework, not a product certification, legal opinion or substitute for testing by a competent laboratory. It does not establish that a particular filter, supplier, process or document set is conforming, and it does not replace the buyer's specification or applicable standards. The final approval decision must rest on the actual article, its production provenance and evidence appropriate to the intended product and market.
