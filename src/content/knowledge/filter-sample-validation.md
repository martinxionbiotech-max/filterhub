A supplier sample is often the first physical evidence a buyer receives. It looks correct, arrives with a datasheet and may be accompanied by a test report. The buyer measures a few dimensions, confirms that the sample fits the drawing and moves toward approval.

That process answers a narrow question: is this sample acceptable? It does not answer the purchasing question: can this supplier produce the same level of quality routinely?

Sample validation must therefore do more than inspect a single unit. It must establish that the sample represents a controlled design, that the performance claims have been tested appropriately and that the supplier has a production system capable of maintaining the approved configuration. For the wider evidence chain, see the [filter quality verification framework](/knowledge/filter-quality-verification/).

## 1. Treat the Sample as a Claim to Be Tested

A sample is not neutral evidence. It is a claim by the supplier that a particular configuration can meet the buyer's requirements. The buyer's job is to define which parts of that claim need independent confirmation and which can be supported by supplier records.

The claim may include:

- Dimensional fit and sealing geometry.
- Media grade, basis weight, fibre construction and treatment.
- Pleat count, pleat depth and effective media area.
- Frame, bond, gasket and support construction.
- Initial pressure drop at a specified airflow.
- Filtration efficiency or classification.
- Dust-holding or loading behaviour.
- Element integrity and leak-tightness.
- Cleanliness, odour, temperature resistance or other application-specific properties.

Not every claim needs a laboratory test. A dimension can be measured with a calibrated gauge. A gasket can be inspected and compression-tested. A media declaration can be reviewed against a specification. But the validation plan should make the basis for each conclusion explicit.

**A sample approval without a test plan becomes a visual preference test.** That is not a reliable supplier qualification.

## 2. Write the Sample Plan Before the Supplier Ships

The buyer should specify what will be supplied, how many units are needed and what will happen to them. A sample programme planned after delivery often runs into destructive-test conflicts, missing traceability or insufficient units for repeatability.

The sample plan should cover:

- Model, size, media and construction requested.
- Production location and line.
- Whether the sample is prototype, pre-production or routine production.
- Number of units for visual, dimensional, non-destructive and destructive tests.
- Test methods and acceptance criteria.
- Whether tests are performed by the supplier, buyer or an independent laboratory.
- Required certificates and raw data.
- Sample retention and identification.
- Schedule for approval and any subsequent production hold.

If the supplier sends a “special” sample made with extra care, the buyer should know. A hand-built sample can be useful for design evaluation, but it is weak evidence of routine capability. If the product is customised, the approved sample should be linked to the drawing and production process that will be used for supply.

## 3. Verify Configuration and Provenance

Before testing performance, verify that the sample is the configuration the buyer intends to buy. A correct-looking filter may differ in media, gasket, frame thickness or internal support.

Record:

- Manufacturer and production site.
- Model and drawing revision.
- Serial or sample identifier.
- Dimensions and tolerances.
- Media and component declarations.
- Photographs of labels, seals and construction.
- Date and batch information, where available.
- Any deviations disclosed by the supplier.

The supplier should explain which components are proprietary and which are controlled by specification. The buyer does not need to own the supplier's intellectual property, but should know enough to detect a meaningful change. As an illustrative example, a change in media supplier or gasket compound can affect performance even if the external dimensions remain identical.

The first series discusses why [media thickness alone does not determine filter performance](/knowledge/air-filter-surface-area-vs-media-thickness/); effective area, pleat geometry and construction also matter. Sample validation should therefore check the construction that creates those characteristics, not only a media label.

## 4. Check Dimensions, Fit and Sealing Interface

Dimensions are one of the few characteristics a buyer can verify directly, so they deserve a disciplined check. Measure the features that affect installation and sealing:

- Overall length, width and depth.
- Frame squareness and flatness.
- Gasket position, thickness and compression characteristics.
- Header or flange dimensions.
- Media pack position within the frame.
- Clearance for housing, clips, tracks and access.

A sample can meet nominal dimensions and still fail to seal if the gasket is hard, misplaced or insufficiently compressed. The buyer should inspect the gasket material, bond and surface condition, and if possible test fit the sample in the actual housing or a representative fixture.

Filters that are installed in arrays or tracks need particular attention to cumulative tolerance. A small dimensional difference can become a gap across a bank of filters. The receiving check should distinguish a sample that fits once from a design that seals reliably in the installed arrangement.

## 5. Inspect Media and Construction by Planned Destructive Examination

Some of the most important quality characteristics are hidden inside the finished filter. Media type, pleat uniformity, bond coverage, support structure and internal damage may require cutting open a sample.

A destructive examination plan should be agreed in advance and should record:

- Pleat count and spacing.
- Pleat depth and uniformity.
- Effective media area or a traceable estimate.
- Media appearance, discolouration and defects.
- Bond line continuity and adhesive placement.
- Frame-to-media attachment.
- Gasket attachment and material.
- Presence of loose material, debris or damage.

The examination should be performed against the approved drawing and sample, not against an idealised picture in a brochure. Photographs are useful, but the record should identify the unit, orientation and location of each observation.

Destructive testing consumes samples. Plan enough units for both the destructive examination and any performance tests that must be performed on an intact element. If the same characteristic can be checked non-destructively, use the non-destructive method first and reserve destructive inspection for a sample selected on a defined basis.

## 6. Test Performance at the Intended Operating Condition

Performance validation should use a method that matches the product type and the buyer's decision. The standard family must be selected before the sample arrives; trying to choose a method after seeing available results invites confirmation bias.

For general ventilation filters, ISO 16890 or ASHRAE 52.2 may be relevant depending on the specification. The two frameworks do not produce interchangeable labels. For high-efficiency filters, EN 1822 or ISO 29463 parts may address classification, media, leakage and element efficiency. For intake-air cleaners, ISO 5011 is relevant; for lubricating-oil filters, the ISO 4548 series applies to defined test types. The buyer should cite the exact part and test object.

Pressure drop should be reported at the intended airflow and with the tested dimensions. Where scaling is used, the basis should be documented. A simple fan-power relationship helps interpret why pressure drop matters:

$$P_{fan} = \frac{Q \, \Delta p}{\eta_f}$$

where \(P_{fan}\) is the air power required, \(Q\) is volumetric airflow, \(\Delta p\) is pressure drop and \(\eta_f\) is fan efficiency. This is a physical relationship, not a prediction for a particular installation. It shows why a lower pressure drop at the same flow is valuable when the fan and system are otherwise comparable.

Efficiency or penetration can be expressed from upstream and downstream concentrations:

$$\eta = 1 - \frac{C_d}{C_u}$$

where \(C_u\) and \(C_d\) are the upstream and downstream concentrations measured by the test method. The calculation is only meaningful when the sampling, challenge and measurement conditions are valid. The buyer should not take a reported percentage without the method that produced it.

## 7. Decide Whether Integrity Testing Is Required

For high-efficiency elements, sample approval should include the integrity evidence appropriate to the product and application. A classification report does not prove that the submitted sample has no leak. A leak scan does not establish the classification efficiency of the design.

The sample plan should state whether the supplier must provide:

- A factory leak-test record for the sample.
- A witnessed scan test.
- An independent element efficiency test.
- A housing or installed-system leak test.
- Media-level test evidence in addition to finished-element data.

The acceptance criterion should be tied to the applicable standard or agreed method. The buyer should also decide how a detected leak is handled: reject the sample, allow repair and retest, or require a new sample. That decision should be made before testing, not negotiated after a failure.

For the distinction between scan testing and classification, see [HEPA and ULPA integrity testing](/knowledge/hepa-ulpa-integrity-testing/). The key point for sample approval is that the test object and the acceptance record must identify the exact sample.

## 8. Use a Sample Size That Supports the Decision

A single sample can reveal a design defect, but it cannot characterise unit-to-unit variation. If the approval decision depends on consistency, the buyer needs more than one unit or a production record that demonstrates control.

For a variable measured on a sample, the required number of observations for a desired precision can be estimated from:

$$n = \left(\frac{z\sigma}{E}\right)^2$$

where \(z\) is the standard normal quantile for the chosen confidence level, \(\sigma\) is the estimated standard deviation and \(E\) is the desired margin of error. This is a planning aid, not a substitute for a statistically designed sampling plan. If \(\sigma\) is unknown, a pilot sample or the supplier's historical data can provide an estimate, but the assumption should be documented.

The buyer should also consider the cost of each test, the destructiveness of the method and the consequence of missing a defect. A larger sample may improve confidence but cannot compensate for a sample that was not randomly selected or a test method that cannot detect the relevant failure mode.

## 9. Check Production Representativeness

A sample approval is only as good as the link between the sample and routine production. Ask the supplier to demonstrate that the sample was made with the same:

- Media and component suppliers.
- Production equipment.
- Process settings.
- Operators and shift arrangements, where relevant.
- Inspection methods.
- Packaging and handling controls.

If the sample was produced on a pilot line, the supplier should explain how the production line differs and what validation will occur before the first shipment. If the sample was selected from a production batch, the batch record should connect the sample to the materials, settings and test results for that batch.

An audit can help, but a sample approval should not depend entirely on a future audit. The buyer needs enough evidence at approval to decide whether to proceed, and enough contractual control to verify the production system afterward. ISO 9001 or ISO 13485 certification may support confidence in that system, but neither certificate proves the performance of the sampled filter.

## 10. Define the Golden Sample and Its Control

An approved sample is often called a golden sample, master sample or reference sample. Its value depends on how it is controlled. A physical sample sitting in a cupboard without identity, storage conditions or change history can create disputes rather than prevent them.

The golden sample record should include:

- Unique sample ID and photographs.
- Drawing and specification revision.
- Supplier, production site and date.
- Material declarations.
- Test reports and acceptance decision.
- Storage conditions and inspection interval.
- Changes approved after the original sample.
- Retention period and disposal authority.

The buyer should have its own retained sample or access to an agreed reference. If the supplier changes the product, the golden sample should not be quietly replaced. The buyer should approve the new configuration and define whether a new sample, partial retest or full qualification is required.

## 11. Use a Validation Matrix, Not a Checklist of Impressions

A validation matrix makes the evidence gaps visible. The following table is illustrative; the actual tests and sample quantities must be set for the specific filter and risk.

| Validation area | Evidence | Destructive? | Decision if not met |
|---|---|---|---|
| Configuration | Drawing, material declaration and photographs | No | Hold approval until identity is confirmed |
| Dimensions and fit | Calibrated measurements and housing trial | No | Reject or require drawing change |
| Construction | Cut-open inspection and bond assessment | Yes | Reject sample or require process correction |
| Performance | Report or witnessed test at intended condition | Usually no | Request retest or alternative design |
| Integrity | Factory record, scan or agreed leak test | No, if test method permits | Reject, repair and retest, or new sample |
| Production representativeness | Batch record and process statement | No | Require production-line sample or audit |
| Consistency | Multiple samples or historical process data | Mixed | Set batch controls or request new data |

The matrix should record who reviewed each item and which evidence was accepted. A blank cell is not a neutral gap; it is an open risk that should be carried into the approval decision.

## 12. Decide What “Approved” Means

Sample approval can be full, conditional or rejected. A conditional approval is useful when some evidence is pending, but only if the conditions are explicit: which production is on hold, which tests must pass, who will verify them and when the approval will be final.

The approval record should state:

- The exact configuration approved.
- The tests performed and their results.
- The evidence accepted from the supplier.
- The evidence not obtained.
- Any limitations on the approval.
- The batch or serial range covered.
- The conditions requiring revalidation.

This record becomes the reference for incoming inspection and change control. It also prevents a future team from assuming that a sample approval covered more than it did.

**The purpose of sample validation is not to create a perfect first unit. It is to establish a controlled starting point for production.** That distinction should shape both the tests and the approval language.

## 13. Avoid Common Sample Approval Shortcuts

Sample programmes often fail for predictable procedural reasons rather than a lack of technical knowledge. The following shortcuts are common and should be challenged before approval.

**Approving from a datasheet plus photographs.** A catalogue image and a nominal specification cannot confirm the construction of the unit that will arrive. At minimum, the buyer should inspect a physical sample from the intended production configuration.

**Using one sample to cover every size.** A family report may be relevant to several sizes, but the technical justification for scaling must be explicit. Gasket geometry, frame stiffness, media area and airflow distribution can differ across a size range. The buyer should know which sizes are covered by the evidence and which need separate validation.

**Measuring dimensions without controlling the gauge.** A caliper reading is only useful if the instrument is suitable, calibrated and used consistently. For critical fits, measure the same features in the same way as the drawing and the housing interface. If the measurement system cannot distinguish a conforming part from a nonconforming one, the inspection creates false confidence.

**Skipping destructive inspection to keep the sample intact.** Preserving a sample for display is not more important than verifying bond coverage, pleat geometry and media construction. The buyer should plan for both retained samples and consumed samples. A non-destructive check cannot always reveal a hidden bond or pleat defect.

**Accepting a result without a production link.** A passing test on a carefully made sample does not show that the next batch will pass. The approval file should identify the production location, line and batch, and the quality agreement should require notification before a material or process change.

**Leaving conditions vague.** Phrases such as “subject to satisfactory sample approval” do not define what satisfactory means. The approval should state the tests, acceptance criteria, evidence owner and any production hold. A conditional approval without a closure condition can drift into permanent acceptance.

These shortcuts are tempting because each one saves time in the short term. Their cost appears later as unresolved deviations, inconsistent incoming batches or disputes over whether the supplier changed the product. A disciplined sample plan is not bureaucratic excess; it is the cheapest point at which to discover that the buyer and supplier have different definitions of the approved product.

## The practical position

The practical position is that a sample should be treated as a manufacturing claim, not a design trophy. We would approve only the configuration the supplier can repeat, and production-line provenance should be a condition of approval rather than a follow-up question. A hand-built unit can settle a design debate, but it should not release supply. If the supplier cannot show the batch record behind the sample, the buyer is still evaluating a prototype. Approval language should make that distinction visible before the purchase order turns it into a commitment.

## 14. What this page does not claim.

This page is a supplier-qualification and sample-validation guide, not a test report, certificate or substitute for the applicable standard. It does not establish that a particular sample, supplier or production process meets any efficiency, pressure-drop, capacity, cleanliness or integrity requirement. Test methods, sample quantities and acceptance criteria must be chosen for the specific product, application and risk, with qualified technical review. Final supplier approval remains the buyer's responsibility and should include independent testing where the consequence of failure warrants it.
